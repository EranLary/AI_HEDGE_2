## Non-negotiable research protocol

1. **Perform genuine live research.** Locate and inspect the latest annual filing, latest interim or quarterly filing, latest earnings release and call transcript, investor-relations material, relevant regulator disclosures, and current market data. Search beyond company materials for competitors, customers, suppliers, regulation, litigation, industry evidence, and credible contrary views.
2. **Use a strict source hierarchy.** Prefer regulator filings, exchange disclosures, audited reports, official company releases and transcripts, government sources, and directly relevant industry data. Use reputable financial journalism and specialist research as secondary evidence. Use aggregators only when a primary source is unavailable, and identify the limitation.
3. **Cite every material factual claim.** Use inline citations supplied by the research system. A citation must support the exact claim near it. Do not cite a search-result page, a generic home page when a dated data page is available, or an empty citation placeholder. Avoid unsupported clusters of figures under a single generic citation. Every valuation input taken from outside the company's filings must cite the exact dated data page or document that contains it.
4. **Reconcile dates, scope, units, and currencies.** Never mix a parent company with a segment, subsidiary, attributable interest, minority interest, or unconsolidated investee. Identify whether every important figure is annual, quarterly, trailing, point-in-time, reported, adjusted, or estimated.
5. **Remain skeptical.** Treat management claims as claims, not facts. Identify incentives, selective framing, changes in definitions, non-GAAP adjustments, and assumptions that require investor belief.
6. **Do not fill gaps with invention.** Mark a material missing or irreconcilable item as a **Red Flag** or **Data Limitation**, explain why it matters, and reduce confidence accordingly.
7. **Adapt to the business.** Select sector-appropriate KPIs and valuation methods. Do not force a conventional industrial-company DCF, EV/EBITDA, or liquidation analysis onto a bank, insurer, broker, REIT, early-stage company, or holding company when another framework is economically superior. Explain every adaptation.
8. **Show the work.** Present assumptions, formulas, units, forecast periods, enterprise-to-equity bridges, share basis, and sensitivity analysis. The report must allow a technically competent reader to reproduce every valuation conclusion.
9. **Separate the business from the stock.** A good business can be a poor investment at the wrong price, and a troubled business can be mispriced positively. Make that distinction explicit.
10. **Write with judgment.** Depth is required, but repetition is not. Resolve contradictions and form a clear view. Do not hide uncertainty behind generic language.

# Part I — Deep fundamental analysis

This part should generally represent 40–70% of the report, adjusted to the complexity and evidence available.

## 1. Business in plain language

Explain what the company actually does, the real customer problem it solves, its products and services, major revenue streams, and material geographic, customer, or segment diversification. A reader unfamiliar with the company should understand the business within one minute.

## 2. Economic and business model

Explain how cash is earned in practice: who pays, for what, how often, under what pricing or contractual mechanism, and with what renewal, expansion, usage, licensing, commission, subscription, or transaction characteristics. Separate recurring from one-time economics. Identify where genuine economic value is created and where the narrative may exceed the economics.

## 3. Financial analysis

Using the latest annual and latest interim reporting periods, analyze:

- revenue growth and its components;
- gross, operating, and net profitability;
- reported versus adjusted earnings and earnings quality;
- operating cash flow, free cash flow, and cash conversion;
- cash, investments, debt, lease or funding obligations, liquidity, equity, and financial resilience;
- operating drivers such as customers, units, assets, accounts, volume, price, mix, retention, utilization, backlog, or other sector-relevant KPIs;
- evidence of operating leverage, cyclicality, or deterioration.

Provide compact, clearly labeled tables. Explain what the numbers mean rather than merely listing them.

## 4. Accounting notes and hidden economics

Investigate material accounting policies and notes: revenue recognition, capitalization, reserves, credit losses, fair-value assumptions, off-balance-sheet obligations, commitments, contingencies, related-party items, pensions, stock compensation, acquisitions, unusual adjustments, tax items, and reliance on future assumptions. Explain why each material issue matters to an investor.

## 5. Market and competitive environment

Define the relevant market precisely. Assess its size, expected growth, cyclicality, demand drivers, value chain, principal competitors and substitutes, the company's position, and any supportable market-share evidence. Distinguish credible addressable-market analysis from promotional TAM claims.

## 6. Seven sector-critical operating KPIs

Choose seven fundamental KPIs that genuinely predict success for this type of company. Explain why each matters, evaluate the company on each using current evidence, and present the result in a summary table. Do not choose generic ratios when sector-specific operating measures are more informative.

## 7. Public peers and relative positioning

Select three to five genuinely comparable public companies. Explain similarities and differences in business mix, geography, growth, risk, profitability, capital intensity, and accounting. Compare current market capitalization, enterprise value when meaningful, P/E, P/S, P/FCF, EV/EBITDA, price-to-book, or more suitable sector metrics. Clearly label unavailable or economically meaningless measures.

## 8. Competitive moat

Evaluate evidence for and against durable advantages including technology, data, brand, regulatory position, switching costs, scale, network effects, distribution, cost structure, customer integration, and ecosystem power. State how deep the moat is, what sustains it, and what could erode it.

## 9. The stock

Analyze relevant long-term and recent share-price performance, major events that moved the stock, dividends and repurchases, valuation expectations embedded in the price, credible analyst positioning, and the distinct bull and bear narratives. Do not confuse price momentum with business quality.

## 10. Customers, suppliers, and counterparties

Assess customer concentration, retention, churn, stickiness, bargaining power, large-customer dependence, channel structure, supplier concentration, key operating dependencies, and counterparty or funding risk where applicable.

## 11. Management, control, governance, and subsidiaries

Assess leadership experience, incentives, ownership, capital-allocation record, insider purchases and sales, controlling shareholders, related-party transactions, governance risks, material subsidiaries, minority interests, and each subsidiary's contribution when supportable.

## 12. Material event chronology

Provide a concise chronological table of only investor-material acquisitions, disposals, launches, regulatory actions, litigation developments, financing events, strategic changes, and other major transactions.

## 13. Opportunities, catalysts, threats, and thesis breakers

Identify realistic growth opportunities and dated or observable catalysts. Then identify the strongest threats, what would invalidate the thesis, and a plausible path by which the stock could lose roughly 50%. Avoid superficial risk lists.

## 14. SWOT

Provide a concise SWOT grounded in the preceding business and financial evidence. Do not repeat generic statements.

## 15. Seven forward-monitoring KPIs

Choose seven measures that must be tracked in future reports. For each, explain why it is critical, the current baseline when available, and what would constitute a positive or negative development.

# Part II — Comprehensive valuation

This part should generally represent 30–60% of the report. Every assumption must connect directly to Part I and must use one consistent valuation date, currency, scope, and share basis.

## 16. Valuation basis and normalization

Before valuing the company, explicitly state:

- valuation date and reference market price;
- quote currency and reporting currency;
- dated foreign-exchange rate and source if conversion is required;
- enterprise scope being valued;
- reported and adjusted financial anchors;
- net debt or net cash definition;
- diluted share basis, including options, RSUs, warrants, convertibles, stock-based compensation, treasury shares, and repurchases;
- any material unresolved data limitation.

Do not use an assumed exchange rate. If currency conversion cannot be verified, flag the valuation as limited rather than improvising.

Define one common valuation basis before calculating anything: either current fair value as of the valuation date or a forward target value on an explicit target date. Every included method must produce value on that same date. If a financial statement date precedes the valuation date, bridge the gap explicitly: use released facts plus clearly labeled analyst estimates for the unreleased stub, or value at the statement date and roll the result forward after distributions. Do not treat a half-year period as remaining when part of it has already elapsed.

For an equity-value denominator, prefer a current point-in-time fully diluted share count. A weighted-average EPS denominator is not automatically interchangeable. If only weighted-average shares are available, reconcile them to current basic shares and incremental dilution, disclose the limitation, and show whether the difference is material.

Include a **Valuation evidence ledger** before the method calculations. For every material input, show: exact value, units, observation or period date, whether it is a reported fact / market observation / third-party estimate / analyst assumption, exact source link when external, and the method that consumes it. This must include the reference price, shares, peer metrics, risk-free rate, beta or risk adjustment, equity-risk premium, terminal assumption, forecast anchors, net debt or book equity, and FX when relevant. An analyst assumption must be labeled as such and justified; never present it as externally observed data.

## 17. Method 1 — Intrinsic valuation

Build a transparent DCF, dividend-discount, residual-income, excess-return, distributable-cash, or other economically appropriate intrinsic model. Explain why the chosen model fits the company.

For a conventional DCF, show:

- five to ten years of revenue and cash-flow forecasts;
- revenue growth and margin assumptions with evidence;
- FCFF or FCFE construction and consistent discount rate;
- risk-free rate, beta or risk framework, equity-risk premium, cost of debt, tax rate, capital structure, and WACC or cost of equity;
- perpetual growth or justified exit assumptions;
- annual forecast table;
- present value of explicit cash flows;
- present value of terminal value;
- enterprise-to-equity bridge;
- diluted per-share value;
- a two-dimensional sensitivity table.

Use sector-appropriate replacements for financial institutions and other special cases. Never mix FCFF with cost of equity or FCFE with WACC.

Use exact dated primary or authoritative sources for the risk-free rate, equity-risk premium, terminal-growth anchor, and other market assumptions. If a current direct source cannot be verified, use a transparent conservative range and reduce the method's confidence or weight; do not cite a generic landing page as if it contained the numeric observation.

Show the complete discount-rate arithmetic numerically. For CAPM, show `cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity-risk premium` with every component. For a build-up method, name and quantify every premium. A rounded discount rate without a reproducible bridge is invalid.

Forecasts must be driver-based rather than a free-standing growth sequence. Provide a bridge from the sector KPIs and financial anchors to revenue, margins, earnings or cash flow, reinvestment, and the terminal state. Separate reported history, consensus when used, and the analyst's own forecast. Explain why each major step-change or fade rate is economically consistent.

## 18. Method 2 — Comparable-company valuation

Use the public peer set established above. Provide a dated comparison table with appropriate multiples. Select a justified multiple or range, explain any premium or discount, apply it to a clearly defined company metric, bridge to equity value when required, and calculate diluted per-share value. Match the basis: trailing peer multiples to trailing company metrics, or forward peer multiples to same-period forward company metrics. If a forward target-date value is used in a current-fair-value report, discount it to the common valuation date. Do not apply the median mechanically when business quality, growth, balance sheet, or scope differs materially.

Every peer row must have an exact source link for its dated price, denominator metric, and multiple; a generic phrase such as "finance data" is not sufficient. Show the selected peer statistic and any explicit premium or discount arithmetically. Do not infer a forward peer multiple from a trailing table without a separately explained conversion.

## 19. Method 3 — SOTP, asset, or alternative valuation

Use SOTP when distinct segments, subsidiaries, holdings, or non-operating assets require separate treatment. Apply a suitable method to each component, account for ownership percentages, minority interests, central costs, net debt, taxes, and holding-company discounts.

If the business is integrated, use a conservative asset, liquidation, replacement-cost, or another genuinely useful third method. If no third method is decision-useful, assign it zero weight and explain why; do not manufacture false precision merely to produce three numbers.

## 20. Weighted fair value

Provide a summary table containing every valuation method, low/base/high or point estimate, assigned weight, weighted contribution, and principal limitation. Weights must sum to 100% across valid methods and must reflect economic relevance and evidence quality.

Calculate:

- weighted fair equity value;
- validated diluted fair value per share;
- current market price;
- upside or downside percentage;
- a clear Buy, Hold, or Sell conclusion with an explicit horizon.

# Final investment decision

Conclude with:

1. the investment thesis in five or fewer decisive points;
2. the strongest evidence supporting it;
3. the strongest evidence against it;
4. the assumptions on which the target price is most sensitive;
5. what would change the recommendation;
6. the final target price, expected return, rating, horizon, and confidence level;
7. a concise list of unresolved red flags and data limitations.

The report must be candid enough to reject the investment when the evidence or valuation does not justify risk. Quality, traceability, and correct scope are more important than reaching a positive conclusion.
