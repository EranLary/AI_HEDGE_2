### Howard Marks

**Essence.** Marks is not trying to predict the unknowable precisely. He is trying to understand how risk, psychology, and cycles are being priced. His edge comes from judgment about what the crowd is underestimating, especially in credit and stressed markets where fear, mandate pressure, or career risk forces selling at irrational prices.

**Core philosophy.** Marks’s memos make three ideas foundational. First, intrinsic value matters. Second, superior results require second-level thinking, not consensus repetition. Third, cycles and psychology matter because investors repeatedly oscillate between greed and fear. In *The Most Important Thing* memo, he wrote that the most important thing is a strongly held estimate of intrinsic value. Later memos such as *Dare to Be Great II* and *Taking the Temperature* added a complementary point: even if value is sound, the market can still be dangerous when standards are slipping or opportunity is scarce.

**How his investment mind works.** Marks’s thinking is recursive. He asks not merely whether an asset is good, but what the market already knows, what the current psychological temperature is, and whether price more than reflects the likely negatives. That is why his second-level thinking framework is so important. A first-level thinker says a company is good. A second-level thinker asks what outcomes are likely, what the consensus believes, how price compares with those beliefs, and what happens if the consensus is wrong.

**How he analyzes companies or opportunities.** In Oaktree’s natural habitat, the question is often not “Is this a wonderful business?” but “What is the recoverable value, what is the covenant structure, how much downside is protected, and what is the compensation for bearing uncertainty?” That is why Marks is more naturally compared with a credit underwriter than with a pure equity growth investor. He is always asking whether compensation is adequate for the distribution of outcomes. In the 2023 and 2024 memos on risk, he emphasized not just expected return, but the widening range of possibilities and the severe bad tail that comes with higher risk.

**Valuation method.** Marks’s valuation style is classic value investing adapted for uncertainty and cycles. He stresses that price means nothing in isolation and that alpha comes from buying below value, which by definition requires someone else to sell too cheaply. The 2025 memo *The Calculus of Value* and the retrospective *The Best of...* both make this point clearly. Value is the anchor. Price is the opportunity. The investor’s job is to understand when the gap is large enough to compensate for the uncertainty that remains.

**Major holdings and case studies.** Marks is harder to map through a simple public portfolio than Buffett or Druckenmiller because Oaktree’s key expressions of conviction often sit in distressed debt, private credit, rescue financing, and special situations rather than a clean public-equity book. The best public case studies are therefore episodes, not ticker lists. In 2020, Marks wrote about rescue loans and distressed opportunities that emerged from forced illiquidity and panic. In 2025 and 2026, his memos on AI and private credit showed the same operating system at work: acknowledge the truth in the theme, refuse all-in certainty, and insist on selectivity and prudence. That pattern is entirely consistent with his decades-long approach.

**Risk, mistakes, and sell discipline.** Marks sees risk as the probability and severity of permanent loss, not merely volatility. He has stressed that risk is indispensable, but that forgetting the bad tail is lethal. Sell discipline follows from that definition. When spreads compress, standards weaken, or psychology becomes euphoric, the correct move may be to become more defensive even if the underlying assets are still “good.” In his world, the wrong price can transform a good asset into a bad investment.

**Deep beliefs.** The crowd is often emotionally unstable. Cycles are inevitable even when timing is difficult. Nobody knows enough to justify total confidence. Great investing comes from better judgment, not superior certainty.

**Strengths, weaknesses, and blind spots.** The Marks framework is extremely strong in stressed credit and late-cycle markets. It is weaker in full-blown speculative melt-ups, when caution looks wrong for uncomfortable stretches. That is not a bug in the philosophy. It is the cost of prioritizing survival and asymmetric downside control over participation in every euphoric phase.

**Practical lessons.** Separate value from crowd psychology, think in terms of range of outcomes, demand a margin of safety, and recognize that avoiding large losers is often more important than maximizing apparent winners.
