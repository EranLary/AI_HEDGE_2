### Warren Buffett

**Essence.** Buffett’s investment operating system is built to maximize long-term growth in per-share intrinsic value by acquiring superior businesses, funding them conservatively, and allocating capital with unusual patience. He began as a Graham-style bargain hunter, but under Munger’s influence evolved toward buying wonderful businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at wonderful prices. He then wrapped that approach inside Berkshire’s uniquely powerful funding structure, especially insurance float.

**Core philosophy.** Buffett’s official letters repeatedly define intrinsic value as the present value of future cash flows, while also warning that the calculation is necessarily imprecise. His owner-related principles emphasize rational market prices, partnership with shareholders, and measuring success by per-share intrinsic value rather than accounting optics alone. His post-1990 writing also makes clear that Berkshire’s real advantage is not just security selection, but the combination of business ownership, capital allocation, and unusually attractive funding sources.

**How his investment mind works.** Buffett starts by simplifying. Is the business understandable? Does it have durable economics? Is management honest and able? Can Berkshire live comfortably with the downside? Can the company redeploy retained earnings at attractive rates, or should capital be returned? His temperament is central: he does not need activity, prediction, or consensus validation. The market is a quoting machine, not an instructor.

**How he analyzes companies or opportunities.** Buffett’s framework centers on moats, owner earnings, pricing power, predictable demand, balance-sheet resilience, and management quality. The moat is not an abstraction. It shows up in sustained excess returns, customer captivity, brand power, low-cost position, or network effects. His letters on GEICO and the insurance businesses also show how he thinks in terms of operational advantage and funding advantage simultaneously. A good insurance business is not just a profit center. It is a source of investable float, but only if underwriting remains disciplined.

**Valuation method.** Buffett is explicit that intrinsic value is discounted future cash flow. In practice, his valuation method is simpler than Damodaran’s but not fundamentally different. He looks at normalized owner earnings, durability, reinvestment needs, and return on incremental capital. He also insists that buybacks are only beneficial when they are value-accretive, a point he made forcefully in the 2022 letter. That means valuation is always relative to intrinsic value per share, never to market applause.

**Major holdings and case studies.** Berkshire’s most recent public 13F for Q1 2026 listed Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, and Bank of America among the largest disclosed holdings. Apple is especially revealing: it shows Buffett’s framework stretching beyond classic low-multiple value into a consumer ecosystem with loyalty, cash generation, and massive repurchases that quietly increase Berkshire’s ownership over time. American Express and Coca-Cola reveal the classic Buffett taste for brand strength, habitual consumer behavior, and long-duration pricing power. GEICO and National Indemnity reveal something even deeper: Buffett’s real masterpiece is not a single stock, but a capital allocation machine funded in meaningful part by float that has often been free or better than free when underwriting is done properly. As of 2026, however, Berkshire’s public portfolio should be read with one caveat: Greg Abel became CEO at the start of 2026 while Buffett remained chairman, so the latest portfolio is Berkshire’s, not a pure window into Buffett alone.

**Risk, mistakes, and sell discipline.** Buffett does not define risk as volatility. His letters repeatedly point instead to permanent loss, leverage, and poor underwriting or bad business economics. In the 2018 letter he explicitly rejected the Russian-roulette logic of debt-fueled return enhancement. Sell discipline, likewise, is sparse. He sells more readily when management or economics disappoint than when price simply rises, because his ideal outcome is often to hold a compounding business for a very long time.

**Deep beliefs.** Capitalism works best through high-trust ownership, rationality, and compounding. Float is extraordinary only if underwriting discipline is religious. Markets are useful when they offer foolish prices and otherwise ignorable. Quality plus time beats cleverness plus turnover.

**Strengths, weaknesses, and blind spots.** Buffett’s strengths are temperament, capital allocation, financing structure, and business simplicity. His blind spots are mostly a function of scale and occasional conservatism around emerging technologies. Berkshire’s size narrows the set of opportunities that can matter. But the system’s durability has come precisely from refusing to abandon discipline for excitement.

**Practical lessons.** Think in per-share intrinsic value, make funding structure part of the analysis, respect balance sheets, treat buybacks analytically rather than ideologically, and understand that inactivity is often a feature, not a flaw.
